The Evolutionary Roots of Cognitive Biases: The Importance of Critical Thinking in Overcoming Biased Reasoning

Benjamin A. Moss · Originally published on LinkedIn

View original on LinkedIn

Download author manuscript PDF

Abstract: This paper explores the evolutionary roots of cognitive biases and their implications for moral reasoning. Drawing on recent research in evolutionary psychology, the paper argues that many cognitive biases may have been evolutionary adaptations that helped our ancestors survive and thrive in their environments. However, these biases can also lead us astray in modern, complex societies where critical thinking skills are necessary for rational and ethical decision-making. The paper discusses several examples of cognitive biases in the moral realm, including the naturalistic fallacy and the just-world fallacy, and argues that critical thinking skills are essential for overcoming these biases and developing more accurate and nuanced perspectives on ethical issues. Ultimately, the paper emphasizes the importance of cultivating critical thinking skills as a means of overcoming biased reasoning and promoting effective moral reasoning.


Introduction:

Human beings have an incredible capacity for rational thought, and our ability to reason and solve complex problems is a defining characteristic of our species. However, despite our cognitive abilities, we are prone to making cognitive errors and engaging in biased thinking. This is particularly true in the realm of moral psychology, where our intuitions and emotions often play a significant role in our moral reasoning (Haidt, 2001).

One reason for this is that our moral intuitions and emotions are shaped by our evolutionary history. For example, disgust is a powerful moral emotion that can lead us to avoid behaviors or situations that we perceive as morally wrong. While this emotion may have helped our ancestors avoid harmful or disease-ridden objects, it can also lead us to be overly judgmental or intolerant of those who engage in behaviors we find disgusting (Rozin, Lowery, Imada, & Haidt, 1999).

Similarly, our tendency to engage in tribalism, or to identify with and prioritize the interests of our own group over others, may have helped our ancestors survive and thrive in small, tightly-knit communities. However, in modern society, this bias can lead to conflicts and discrimination against out-groups (Haidt, 2012).

These cognitive biases and evolutionary adaptations can affect our moral reasoning in significant ways. For example, the naturalistic fallacy, or the belief that what is natural is inherently good, can lead us to make judgments about what is right or wrong based on our intuitions about what is natural (Greene, 2013). This fallacy may have been adaptive in our evolutionary past, as natural environments and behaviors were often beneficial for our survival. However, in modern society, it can lead to biased or harmful judgments about individuals or groups who engage in behaviors deemed "unnatural."

Overcoming these biases and engaging in critical thinking is essential for effective moral reasoning. By examining our intuitions and emotions critically and considering alternative perspectives, we can better understand the root causes of our biases and work to overcome them (Kunda, 1990). Additionally, developing critical thinking skills such as analyzing evidence, evaluating arguments, and identifying logical fallacies can help us to engage in motivated reasoning, or the process of using reason and evidence to support our beliefs rather than simply relying on intuition or emotion (Stanovich & West, 2008).

While our cognitive abilities are impressive, we are also prone to making cognitive errors and engaging in biased thinking, particularly in the realm of moral psychology. Understanding the evolutionary roots of our biases and developing critical thinking skills are essential for effective moral reasoning and decision-making.

This essay is organized into four sections. The introduction discusses the tendency of humans to engage in biased thinking and how it is influenced by evolutionary history. Section 1 explores how cognitive biases can be seen as evolutionary adaptations that helped our ancestors survive and thrive. Section 2 delves into how cognitive biases affect moral psychology and can lead to harmful beliefs and behaviors. Section 3 highlights the importance of critical thinking skills in overcoming cognitive biases and engaging in rational and ethical decision-making. Finally, the section 4 discusses possible objections and replies to my thesis. The essay ends with the assertion that understanding the evolutionary roots of cognitive biases can help in recognizing and overcoming them. It is essential to develop critical thinking skills to ensure more accurate and nuanced perspectives on ethical issues.



Section 1: The Evolutionary Basis of Cognitive Biases


Recent research in evolutionary psychology suggests that cognitive biases may have been evolutionary adaptations that helped our ancestors survive and thrive in their environments. For example, confirmation bias, the tendency to seek out and prioritize information that confirms our existing beliefs, may have helped our ancestors make quick decisions in uncertain and dangerous situations. A study by Kunda (1990) found that participants who were given ambiguous information about a fictitious person were more likely to interpret the information in a way that confirmed their pre-existing beliefs about that person. This suggests that confirmation bias may have been a useful cognitive tool for our ancestors in situations where quick decision-making was necessary for survival.

Similarly, the availability heuristic, which involves relying on readily available information to make judgments, may have helped our ancestors avoid danger by quickly recognizing and responding to threats. A study by Tversky and Kahneman (1973) found that participants were more likely to judge an event as likely to occur if it was more easily remembered, even if the actual probability of the event was low. This suggests that the availability heuristic may have been an effective way for our ancestors to quickly assess potential threats in their environment.

However, while these cognitive biases may have been helpful in the past, they can also lead us astray in modern, complex societies where critical thinking skills are necessary for rational and ethical decision-making. Therefore, it is important to recognize and overcome these biases through the cultivation of critical thinking skills, as discussed earlier in this paper.


Section 2: Biased Reasoning in Moral Psychology


However, these cognitive biases can also lead us astray in the moral realm. For example, the naturalistic fallacy, which is the belief that what is natural is morally good, may be a product of our evolutionary history (Brosnan & de Waal, 2014). Our ancestors may have been more likely to trust natural remedies and behaviors in the absence of scientific knowledge. This tendency may have been adaptive in the past, as it would have helped our ancestors survive in their environment. However, in modern times, the naturalistic fallacy can lead to harmful beliefs and behaviors, such as the rejection of vaccines or the belief that certain groups of people are inherently superior or inferior based on natural characteristics.

Similarly, the just-world fallacy, which is the belief that people get what they deserve, may also be a product of our evolutionary history (Lerner & Miller, 1978). Our ancestors may have been more likely to reward cooperation and punish non-cooperation, as this would have helped to maintain group cohesion and ensure survival. However, in modern times, the just-world fallacy can lead to victim blaming and the belief that people who experience hardship or injustice somehow brought it upon themselves.

Overall, understanding the evolutionary roots of cognitive biases can help us recognize and overcome them in our moral reasoning, and develop more accurate and nuanced perspectives on ethical issues.


Section 3: The Importance of Critical Thinking in Overcoming Biased Reasoning


Despite the evolutionary roots of cognitive biases, it is important to recognize and overcome them in order to engage in rational and ethical decision-making. This requires the development of critical thinking skills, which involve questioning assumptions, evaluating evidence, and considering alternative perspectives (Paul & Elder, 2006). By cultivating these skills, individuals can learn to recognize and overcome cognitive biases, engage in motivated reasoning, and make more informed and ethical decisions.

Research suggests that critical thinking skills can be taught and developed through various educational interventions (Halpern, 2014). For example, students can be taught to evaluate evidence and arguments, identify logical fallacies, and recognize the influence of biases and values on reasoning. These interventions have been shown to improve students' ability to think critically, make informed decisions, and engage in ethical reasoning (Halpern, 2014).

Moreover, critical thinking skills are particularly important in the realm of moral psychology, where emotions and intuitions can often cloud our judgment and lead us astray. By questioning our assumptions and biases, evaluating evidence, and considering alternative perspectives, we can develop more nuanced and accurate moral reasoning and make more informed and ethical decisions (Paul & Elder, 2006).

In conclusion, while cognitive biases may have evolutionary roots, it is essential to recognize and overcome them through the development of critical thinking skills. By doing so, individuals can engage in motivated reasoning and make more informed and ethical decisions.


Section 4: Objections and Replies: 

Counterargument 1: Cognitive biases are not exclusively evolutionary adaptations

While it is true that some cognitive biases have an evolutionary basis, not all of them do. Some cognitive biases may arise from societal or cultural factors rather than evolutionary adaptations. For example, the "confirmation bias" may arise from the desire to confirm pre-existing beliefs rather than any evolutionary imperative.

Response: While it is true that not all cognitive biases are evolutionary adaptations, it is important to note that many of our cognitive biases do have an evolutionary basis. For example, the "negativity bias" may have arisen as an evolutionary adaptation to help us avoid potential dangers and threats (Rozin & Royzman, 2001). Therefore, understanding the evolutionary basis of cognitive biases can still be valuable in helping us understand and overcome them.

Counterargument 2: Critical thinking may not always overcome cognitive biases

While critical thinking is an important tool for overcoming cognitive biases, it may not always be effective. Research has shown that even when people are explicitly told to use critical thinking, they may still fall victim to cognitive biases (Lilienfeld, Ruscio, & Beyerstein, 2010).

Response: While it is true that critical thinking may not always be effective in overcoming cognitive biases, it is still an important tool to have. By cultivating critical thinking skills, we can increase our awareness of potential biases and become better equipped to recognize and overcome them. Additionally, studies have shown that critical thinking training can lead to improvements in reasoning skills and decision-making (Abrami et al., 2015). Therefore, while it may not always be a foolproof solution, critical thinking is still a valuable tool for overcoming cognitive biases.


Conclusion:

In conclusion, cognitive biases are deeply ingrained in human reasoning and have evolutionary roots that were instrumental in our ancestors' survival and success. While these biases may have been adaptive in the past, they can lead to flawed moral reasoning and decision-making in the present. To avoid falling victim to cognitive biases, it is crucial to develop critical thinking skills that involve questioning assumptions, evaluating evidence, and considering alternative perspectives. By engaging in motivated reasoning, individuals can overcome cognitive biases and make more informed and ethical decisions. Ultimately, the cultivation of critical thinking skills is essential to creating a fair and equitable society where individuals are capable of making moral and ethical choices that promote the common good.
