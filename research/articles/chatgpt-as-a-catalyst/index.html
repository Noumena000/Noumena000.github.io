ChatGPT as a Catalyst for Critical Thinking: Rethinking AI’s Role in the Classroom

Benjamin A. Moss · Originally published on LinkedIn

View original on LinkedIn

By Benjamin Moss

Amid the excitement—and anxiety—surrounding generative AI, educators face a pivotal question: Will tools like ChatGPT undermine student thinking, or can they help cultivate it?

Much of the conversation around AI in education centers on academic dishonesty and intellectual outsourcing. But what if we’re asking the wrong questions? What if the real opportunity lies in using AI not to replace reasoning, but to refine it?

As a philosophy professor teaching ethics and logic, I’ve spent the past year experimenting with AI in the classroom. The results have convinced me of a powerful insight: when guided by philosophical pedagogy, AI can become a surprising ally for critical thinking.

🔍 The Philosophical Core: Thinking About Thinking

Critical thinking, at its root, is an epistemic activity. It’s not merely about forming opinions—it’s about cultivating beliefs that are justified, coherent, and responsibly held. This distinction, emphasized from Plato to Descartes to Gettier, is more relevant than ever in the age of algorithmic fluency.

ChatGPT can generate coherent arguments. But coherence is not the same as truth. Students who treat AI responses as reliable simply because they “sound good” risk falling into what I call automated dogmatism—accepting unverified claims without questioning their assumptions, sources, or logic.

That’s why educators must frame AI not as a knowledge engine, but as an epistemic interlocutor. Used wisely, AI can provoke deeper questioning: Why does this argument sound persuasive? Is it valid, or merely plausible? What’s missing?

This is not just critical thinking—it’s philosophy in action.

🧠 Logic and Language: AI as a Critical Lens

One of the most effective uses of ChatGPT in my classroom has been in logic instruction. Students take AI-generated arguments and translate them into standard form—identifying premises, conclusions, and hidden assumptions.

Why does this matter? Because logic reveals the structure of reasoning beneath the surface of fluent prose. It allows students to detect fallacies, challenge rhetorical illusions, and distinguish valid inference from verbal polish.

This practice turns AI into a cognitive x-ray machine: a tool for making the invisible structure of arguments visible, and therefore challengeable.

⚖️ Ethics, Autonomy, and Intellectual Virtue

AI can simulate ethical reasoning—but it does not understand value. It has no stake in the ideas it produces. That’s why it’s so powerful as a mirror for student thinking.

When students critique an AI-generated argument about euthanasia, for example, they’re forced to distinguish between the appearance of insight and the presence of actual moral reasoning. They must decide what matters—not just what sounds persuasive.

This cultivates intellectual humility—the ability to recognize one’s own fallibility and revise beliefs accordingly. In a time of increasing polarization and misinformation, that’s not just an academic virtue. It’s a civic one.

🗣️ ChatGPT as Dialectical Partner

In my applied ethics courses, I’ve invited students to prompt ChatGPT to argue from moral frameworks they don’t personally hold. For example, a student might prompt: “Give me a Kantian argument against physician-assisted suicide.”

The AI responds. Then the student critiques it, revises it, or refutes it.

The point isn’t whether the AI is “right”—it’s that students are practicing dialectical reasoning. They’re confronting unfamiliar views, strengthening their own arguments, and learning to reason in public.

This is what Socratic dialogue looks like in the age of AI.

🛠️ Scaffolding Revision and Metacognition

AI also supports what I call the “visible mind” approach to writing. Students draft an argument, ask ChatGPT for feedback, and then explain their revisions:

  • What did you change?

  • Why?

  • Did the AI help—or mislead?

This reflection isn’t just about editing. It’s about epistemic responsibility—owning your reasoning, your sources, and your conclusions. When students begin to think about their thinking, the learning sticks.

🧱 Visualization and Argument Mapping

Another underexplored affordance of AI is argument mapping. Students can prompt ChatGPT to extract the logical structure of a paragraph, then visualize it using tools like Kialo or MindMup.

This allows students to see the architecture of thought—how claims support one another, where reasoning breaks down, and where opposing views can be integrated. In a world of surface-level persuasion, this practice fosters structural awareness.

⚠️ What Could Go Wrong? Three Key Risks

  1. Cognitive Overreliance

  2. Epistemic Hallucinations

  3. Erosion of Autonomy

The solution is not to ban AI—but to design around these risks.

🧭 Design Principles for Responsible Use

Based on my classroom work, I recommend three core principles for using AI to support critical thinking:

  1. Cognitive Friction

  2. Transparent Mediation

  3. Dialogical Co-Agency

🧪 From Theory to Practice

Here are a few activities that have worked in my classes:

  • Ethics Dialogue: Prompt AI to argue both sides of a moral issue. Have students critique the structure and write rebuttals.

  • Logic Testing: Feed invalid arguments into ChatGPT. Ask it to explain the fallacy, then prompt students to evaluate its analysis.

  • Revision Cycle: Students draft, prompt the AI for feedback, then justify their revisions in writing. This externalizes metacognition.

These exercises don’t outsource thinking—they expose, stretch, and sharpen it.

🧩 Conclusion: Teaching Students to Think Beyond AI

Philosophy reminds us that thinking is hard—and that’s precisely why it’s valuable. Tools like ChatGPT can support this process, but only if we use them with care, critique, and clarity.

The goal isn’t just to teach students how to think with AI. It’s to empower them to think beyond it.
